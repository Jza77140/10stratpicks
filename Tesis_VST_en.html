1 Business model

Vistra is a merchant power generator integrated with a retail electricity business. As of December 31, 2025 its fleet totaled 43,641 MW: gas 26,989 MW (62%), coal 8,743 MW (20%), nuclear 6,448 MW (15%) and solar plus batteries 1,274 MW (3%). It sells that output into the ERCOT, PJM, ISO-NE and California wholesale markets, and in parallel sells at retail —TXU Energy and other brands— from California to Maine. The two legs hedge each other: retail sells at a fixed price and buys power, so when wholesale rises the retail margin compresses and when it falls the margin expands. Second quarter 2026 shows it: Texas went from $142M to $311M of adjusted EBITDA and East from $418M to $642M, while Retail barely moved from $756M to $773M —and over the half year retail fell from $940M to $841M. Ongoing operations adjusted EBITDA for the quarter was $1,767M, up 31% year over year. Careful with the revenue line: the quarter's $4,017M was DOWN from $4,250M because it embeds $472M of unrealized hedging losses, which is why net income fell to $305M from $327M while EBITDA rose 31%. The raw's $18,985M of LTM revenue is consistent with the half year ($9,657M) plus the rest of the year, but it is a line contaminated by mark-to-market, not a measure of the business. Two of the raw's numbers do not reconcile and one does: a $46,345M market cap at $149.30 implies 310.4 million shares, but the second-quarter 10-Q cover reports 335,635,195 shares as of August 3, 2026 —real market cap is around $50,100M and the true P/E is roughly 24.7x on income attributable to common stock, not 23.3x. By contrast, LTM net income of $2,219M reconciles exactly against EDGAR (652+233+1,029+305) and so does the 2.96x ND/EBITDA (approximately $19,500M of net debt over $6,584M of LTM adjusted EBITDA).

Growth drivers: Vistra is converting merchant capacity into 20-year contracted cash flow. In January 2026 it signed agreements with Meta for 2,609 MW of nuclear power in PJM: 1,268 MW from Perry, 908 MW from Davis-Besse and 433 MW of uprates at Perry, Davis-Besse and Beaver Valley; deliveries start December 2026 (Perry) and December 2027 (Davis-Besse), with the uprates only completing in 2034. With AWS it holds a 20-year PPA for 1,200 MW from Comanche Peak, delivering from the fourth quarter of 2027 and ramping to full capacity by 2032. On the gas side it bought seven plants from Lotus Infrastructure for $1,900M —closed in May 2025, contributing three months to the quarter— and agreed to acquire Cogentrix: 5,500 MW across PJM, ISO-NE and ERCOT for roughly $2,300M in cash, 5,000,000 shares valued at $185, and the assumption of about $1,500M of debt; FERC has approved and closing is expected mid-to-late 2026. It is also building two gas units in the Permian Basin, developing the Oak Hill 2 and Pulaski solar projects, and has committed up to $1,000M to Helix Digital Infrastructure alongside KKR, NVIDIA and the Kuwait Investment Authority, where it stands as preferred power provider. The uncomfortable detail: the 2027 adjusted EBITDA "midpoint opportunity" of $7,400M-$7,800M excludes Cogentrix and the Meta PPAs, and was calculated on market curves as of October 31, 2025 that have since fallen. And beware the easy comparison the raw offers: the 5-year median P/E of 57.7x is not an anchor, it is an artifact —over that window Vistra lost money in 2021 (−$1,274M) and 2022 (−$1,227M), so the median is computed on broken or near-zero denominators. The 13.4x NTM P/E is not cheap "versus 57.7x"; it is cheap or expensive versus the $7,400M-$7,800M of 2027 EBITDA, which is the only number worth auditing.

3 Competitive advantage (MOAT)

Vistra plays in two markets with opposite rules. ERCOT is "energy-only": there is no capacity payment, profitability comes from scarcity peaks, and the battery wave —the US is adding on the order of 24 GW of storage in 2026 alone— flattens precisely those peaks; that is why ERCOT forward curves have fallen and management already conceded that 2027 points to the low end of the range. PJM is the opposite: the 2028/2029 capacity auction cleared at the regulated cap of $325/MW-day for a third consecutive year and still came up 6,821 MW short of its reliability requirement. Direct competitors are Constellation (largest competitive nuclear fleet, larger still after Calpine), NRG and Talen. The moat is moderate, and it is uneven. The electron is fungible: nobody pays more for a Vistra MWh than for an NRG one. What is scarce is something else —capacity already interconnected, permitted and sited near the load— and there Vistra holds 43,641 MW that cannot be replicated on the timeline data centers need power. The contracted piece, roughly 3,800 MW of nuclear committed for 20 years to Meta and AWS, has a genuine moat; the merchant piece exposed to ERCOT has almost none. The historical verdict is signed by the 5-year ROIC: 4.7%, below any reasonable cost of capital. That figure is distorted by the 2021 (winter storm Uri) and 2022 losses, but the point stands: as a pure merchant generator Vistra never durably earned its cost of capital, and the bull case rests on the 20-year PPAs changing that nature.
Capacity already interconnected
43,641 MW connected and permitted. The barrier is not the turbine: it is interconnection, permitting and siting near the load, which take years and are not available today.
Licensed nuclear fleet
6,448 MW at Comanche Peak, Perry, Davis-Besse and Beaver Valley. Firm 24-hour carbon-free power, unreplicable on the timeline of AI demand.
Natural generation-retail hedge
Retail cushions wholesale: over the half year it fell from $940M to $841M while Texas and East rose sharply. It smooths the cycle, it does not remove it.
20-year hyperscaler contracts
2,609 MW with Meta and 1,200 MW with AWS convert spot-exposed volume into contracted cash flow with investment-grade counterparties.
Financial scale to hedge
$6,295M of available liquidity as of June 30, 2026 lets it collateralize hedges on roughly 100% of 2026 generation and about 94% of 2027. A small generator cannot post that collateral.
