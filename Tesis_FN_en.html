1 Business model

Fabrinet is a contract manufacturer: it does not design or sell its own products, it assembles its customers'. Its specialty is precision optical packaging and alignment —data center transceivers, telecom components, industrial lasers and sensors— at its plants in Chonburi and Pinehurst (Thailand), plus Santa Clara, China, Israel and the UK. The design and the intellectual property belong to the customer; Fabrinet supplies process engineering, cleanrooms, qualification and labor, and bills essentially cost plus a thin margin. In the year ended June 26, 2026 (Fabrinet's fiscal year closes in June, not December) it sold $4,641.1M, up 35.7% year over year, with gross profit of $556.5M: a 12.0% gross margin and EBIT of $462.9M, 10.0% of sales. The latest quarter (Q4 FY2026) posted $1,316M, up 45% year over year, with a 12.2% gross margin and non-GAAP EPS of $4.10. The model consumes balance sheet: Fabrinet buys the components and carries the customer's inventory, and at year-end it held $1,021.2M of inventories and $1,017.9M of receivables —together 52% of its $3,911.0M of total assets—. Net income was $473.0M ($13.05 per diluted share), but $56.7M of that, 10.2% of pre-tax income, is a non-cash mark-up of a private stake bought for $32.4M and now carried at $89.1M. It ends the year with $875.1M in cash and short-term investments and no financial debt.

The fragile assumption: that capacity is built with Fabrinet's money and contracted on a cost-plus basis, with no long-term volume commitments from the customer; if AI optics order flow cools, the plant stays on Fabrinet's side and the volume on the customer's.

3 Competitive advantage (MOAT)

Electronics contract manufacturing is a scale-and-cost-discipline business where Jabil, Sanmina, Celestica, Benchmark and Flex compete, alongside customers' own backward integration and Chinese optical vendors. Fabrinet is not a generic assembler: it specializes in active optical alignment at sub-micron tolerances, engineering- and skilled-labor-intensive work accumulated over 25 years in Thailand, and qualifying a new line takes the customer quarters because it must then requalify the product with its own end customers. That earns a moderate moat, not a strong one, and the evidence comes from the filing itself. First, price: gross margin is 12.0% and it keeps compressing as the company grows —12.35% in FY2024, 12.09% in FY2025, 11.99% in FY2026— exactly the opposite of what a business with pricing power would do. Second, the empirical test: NVIDIA went from 35.1% of revenue in FY2024 to 27.6% in FY2025 and 16.3% in FY2026; in dollars, from about $1,012M to about $757M, a 25% decline while total revenue grew 61%, and it also dropped off the list of customers above 10% of accounts receivable, where it stood at 25.5% a year earlier. A strong moat does not lose a quarter of its largest customer's dollars in two fiscal years. What Fabrinet does have, and it is not trivial, is the position of the world's largest optics-focused contract manufacturer, a Thai cost base that is hard to replicate and a debt-free balance sheet that lets it finance the customer's working capital: that puts it on the short list of three or four suppliers, it does not let it set the price.
